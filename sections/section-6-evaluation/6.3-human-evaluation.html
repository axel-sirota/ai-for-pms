SECTION 6.3

Human Evaluation

The Gold Standard (When Done Right)

The 5-Star Disaster

E-commerce company, 2023: An AI product description generator scored 4.8/5.0 on automated quality metrics - fluency, grammar, and keyword density all excellent. The team shipped it to production. Within a week, returns spiked 23%. The descriptions were technically perfect but subtly misleading - describing features the products didn't have. Automated metrics measured how well it was written, not whether it was true. Only human reviewers caught the difference.

Why Humans Still Matter

Automated metrics can tell you if the output is correct. Only humans can tell you if it's good. A response can be factually accurate but unhelpful, grammatically perfect but offensive, complete but confusing. Human judgment catches what algorithms miss.

The Inter-Rater Reliability Problem

    O[Same Output] --> J1[Judge 1: 4/5]
    O --> J2[Judge 2: 2/5]
    O --> J3[Judge 3: 5/5]
    J1 --> Q[Which is right?]
    J2 --> Q
    J3 --> Q

    style Q fill:#fef2f2,stroke:#dc2626

The Problem: Humans disagree. Two people looking at the same AI output might give completely different ratings. Without measuring this disagreement, your evaluation data is unreliable.

Measuring Agreement: Fleiss' Kappa

Kappa Score Interpretation Action
< 0.2 Poor agreement Rubric is broken - redesign it
0.2 - 0.4 Fair agreement Add examples, calibrate raters
0.4 - 0.6 Moderate agreement Acceptable for most use cases
0.6 - 0.8 Substantial agreement Good - your rubric is working
> 0.8 Near-perfect agreement Excellent - rare in subjective tasks
PM Decision Point

Before trusting human evaluation results, always measure inter-rater reliability:

  • Have 10-20% of samples rated by multiple people
  • Calculate Kappa or % agreement
  • If Kappa < 0.4, fix your rubric before continuing
  • Provide calibration examples to all raters

Designing a Good Rubric

Good Rubric

  • 5 = Completely answers question with accurate info
  • 4 = Answers question with minor omissions
  • 3 = Partially answers, some relevant info
  • 2 = Attempts to answer but mostly misses
  • 1 = Doesn't address the question at all

Clear, specific, observable criteria

Bad Rubric

  • 5 = Excellent
  • 4 = Good
  • 3 = Average
  • 2 = Below average
  • 1 = Poor

Vague, subjective, no examples

Scaling Human Evaluation

The Sampling Strategy

You can't review everything. Smart sampling gets you 90% of the value at 10% of the cost:

  • Random sample: 2-5% of all outputs for quality tracking
  • Stratified sample: Ensure coverage of different use cases
  • Edge case sample: Focus on low-confidence or unusual inputs
  • Failure sample: Deep dive on outputs flagged by automated systems

How to Explain Human Evaluation to Stakeholders

"We have humans rate a sample of AI outputs - like quality inspectors on a factory floor. We check that our raters agree with each other (inter-rater reliability), then use their ratings to calibrate our automated systems. It's expensive to rate everything, so we use strategic sampling - focusing on edge cases and random checks."
